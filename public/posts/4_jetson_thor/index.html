Intro

NVIDIA recently released their latest state-of-the-art chip in their Jetson line of products, the Jetson Thor. It’s impressive enough that it has awoken me from my slumber long enough to do a brief overview of its capabilities. Following the trend of its predecessors, Jetson Thor marketed as an entire platform aimed at bringing interactive artificial intelligence into the physical plane.

Hardware and Microarchitecture

Blackwell GPU

The Jetson Thor T5000 features an NVIDIA Blackwell architecture GPU. The Blackwell architecture was announced March 2024 and is used in their groundbreaking GB200 NVL72 data center chips and their more accessible RTX 50 series. It boasts a custom 4NP process node, CUDA cores with upgraded compute capability, new fifth-generation tensor cores, fourth-generation ray tracing cores, and introduces us to the AI Management Processor (AMP).

The AMP is a RISC-V processor that utilizes Hardware-Accelerated GPU Scheduling (HAGS), which was Microsoft’s answer to the scheduling bottleneck between the CPU and the GPU, empowering gamers and AI-powered rendering apps. I’m not super familiar with this particular chip and, at the moment, it’s not entirely clear to me how it fits in with the rest of the Jetson Thor’s functionalities. I can’t find any direct reference to it in the maze of NVIDIA’s Jetson webpages. I can only assume it is meant to enhance the video output experience in some way.

Even non-technical folks should at least flip through the microarchitecture of these chips since it essentially determines a processor’s capabilities at the level right above the hardware itself. An impressive SiFive processor with vector registers and vector ALUs is only useful when paired with the RISC-V ISA that implements the vector extension. Simply put, it’s an important consideration when looking for a chip with competitive functionality and long-term support.

Everything Else

As for the rest of the Jetson Thor chip, we have an impressive multicore ARM Neoverse CPU, a line of CPUs made for datacenters and edge computing. There’s also dedicated hardware for encoding and decoding impressive quality videos.

The Programmable Vision Accelerator v3 (PVA) is a programmable multi-core Digital Processing Chip (DSP) meant to offload certain computer vision tasks from the GPU.

The Holoscan Sensor Bridge is FPGA chip that implements sensor-over-Ethernet technology, enabling faster multi-sensor transportation of data to the GPU. As a result, robots using this chip will have sharper “reflexes” as they process input data faster.

More info on the exact specs such as USB ports, drive support, video encoder/decoder, etc for the Jetson Thor can be found here.

Software Tools and Ecosystem

The main draw of Jetson line of products, in my opinion, is the advanced software ecosystem, designed to transfer and process data fast.

Along with the Jetpack SDK for developing on the Jetson platform, we also have the Holoscan SDK to directly work with the Holoscan Sensor Bridge. The Holoscan SDK pairs with the Holoscan Sensor bridge by allowing developers to build directed graphs of “operators” to define data processing and ML workflows. NVIDIA also gives us the HoloHub, a repository of reference applications using the Holoscan SDK in a variety of fields on the Holoscan platform.

NVIDIA Metropolis is NVIDIA’s vision AI platform for the development and deployment of automations that rely on vision processing. This platform provides the tools for advanced applications on both the edge and the cloud by combining sensor and visual data for model training and tuning.

Roboticists will likely appreciate the NVIDIA Isaac Platform. Isaac provides its own extension of ROS2 (Robotic Operating System). On this foundation, Isaac also provides the Isaac Manipulator and the Isaac Perceptor. These are collections of CUDA-accelerated libraries and AI models to help develop applications for robotic manipulators and autonomous mobile robots respectively.

Isaac’s final jewel is Isaac GR00T. GR00T is the umbrella term for both the research initiative and the platform for developing foundational models for general purpose robots and humanoid robots. It provides foundation models such as the N1, a family of pretrained customizable models for general robotics applications.

GR00T also comes with data collection and generation workflows for collecting human demonstration data and generating unique simulations for further training.

Jetson Thor serves as the “brain” of Isaac robots with all of the previously mentioned hardware and software for sensor data collection, data processing, and raw computational power. Investors and research companies like Figure AI will find this extremely useful as they continue plow ahead in the race for adaptive humanoid robots.

“Competition” and Alternatives

“Competition” is in quotes here because it’s hard to find a exact one-to-one competitor in the field of edge robotics. Some similar products to Blackwell’s Tensor Cores include Google’s Edge TPU (Tensor Processing Unit). As the name indicates, the processing unit is optimized for matrix multiplication and tensor operations, both fundamental to any machine learning task. NVIDIA’s Tensor Cores are far more general purpose, making more useful in robotics and multi-modal workloads.

There’s also Tenstorrent with their RISC-V Blackhole Tensix Processor featuring “Tensix Cores” and their own software ecosystems. Their full line of products is too long to get into here but their IP ranges from superscalar processors for datacenters to smaller chips that are meant to compete with GPUs for ML tasks. However, to my knowledge, their chips aren’t marketed with a focus on robotics. Jetson Thor goes to great lengths to provide multimedia input capabilities for sensor inputs and a vision accelerator, both crucial for interactive AI.

Conclusion

Edge computing is worth caring about if you enjoy the usefulness of AI but also value your privacy. The field of robotics is the next step into making AI actually useful for our daily lives.
Finally, the chip itself is worth caring about because it’s not easy to set up a fully integrated ecosystem of sensors, a working ROS environment, and readily available multimodal AI models that can run on limited hardware.

I may have spent most of this post talking about features that are also present in the predecessors to Jetson Thor. However, Jetson Thor chip shows remarkable improvements in both compute power, energy efficiency, and edge AI capabilities proving that the age of general robotics is hardly a pipe dream.
